# ITC-01-LES-04 — Why Countries and Businesses Trade

**Level:** Foundation to intermediate | **Study time:** 60–90 minutes  
**Languages:** English, Hindi and Thai

## 1. Learning outcomes
Explain major economic and commercial reasons for trade, distinguish comparative advantage from absolute advantage, and evaluate trade decisions beyond headline price.

## 2. Main reasons for trade
1. **Resource differences:** Countries have different climates, minerals, energy sources, land and labour availability.
2. **Specialisation:** Firms and economies focus on activities where they are relatively more productive.
3. **Comparative advantage:** A party can produce a good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another party, even if it is not absolutely more productive in every activity.
4. **Economies of scale:** Larger production volumes may reduce average cost.
5. **Technology and knowledge:** Firms access capabilities, designs, equipment or expertise unavailable locally.
6. **Consumer choice:** Trade expands variety, quality and seasonal availability.
7. **Market access:** Exporting allows firms to reach more customers and diversify revenue.
8. **Supply-chain resilience:** Multiple qualified sources can reduce dependence, although global sourcing can introduce new risks.

## 3. Absolute vs comparative advantage
- **Absolute advantage:** Producing more output with the same resources, or the same output with fewer resources.
- **Comparative advantage:** Producing at a lower opportunity cost.

### Simple worked example
In one workday:
- Country A can make 10 units of rice or 5 units of cloth.
- Country B can make 6 units of rice or 6 units of cloth.

Opportunity cost:
- A: 1 cloth costs 2 rice; 1 rice costs 0.5 cloth.
- B: 1 cloth costs 1 rice; 1 rice costs 1 cloth.

B has comparative advantage in cloth (lower cost: 1 rice vs 2 rice). A has comparative advantage in rice (0.5 cloth vs 1 cloth). Mutually beneficial specialisation and exchange may be possible if the exchange rate falls between their opportunity costs and other costs/risks do not eliminate the benefit.

## 4. Business sourcing decision: landed cost
Do not compare suppliers using unit price alone. Estimate total landed cost:
- Product price
- Packing and inland transport
- Export handling and documentation
- International freight and insurance, where applicable
- Import duties and taxes
- Customs brokerage and port/terminal charges
- Inspection, storage and last-mile delivery
- Financing, currency conversion and risk allowance

Some cost responsibilities depend on the contract and Incoterms® rule. Avoid double counting costs already included in the supplier's price.

## 5. Strategic trade-offs
Trade can increase choice and efficiency, but creates exposure to:
- Supplier concentration
- Currency volatility
- Long lead times
- Geopolitical or regulatory change
- Quality inconsistency
- Port disruption and transport capacity constraints
- Ethical, environmental and labour risks

## 6. Practical lab
A buyer receives two offers for the same specification:
- Supplier A: lower unit price, longer lead time, single factory.
- Supplier B: higher unit price, shorter lead time, documented quality controls and a second production site.

Build a weighted scorecard with criteria: landed cost, lead time, quality, capacity, compliance, payment risk and continuity. Assign weights totalling 100%, score each supplier 1–5, explain evidence and record assumptions. Do not choose solely from the numerical score if a mandatory compliance gate fails.

## 7. Knowledge check
1. What is comparative advantage based on? **Opportunity cost.**
2. Is the lowest quoted price always the cheapest option? **No; compare landed cost and risk.**
3. Name three reasons firms trade. **Market access, specialisation, technology, scale, resource access or variety.**
4. What should happen if a supplier fails a mandatory product-compliance check? **Stop/hold selection and escalate; do not offset a legal failure with a high price score.**
